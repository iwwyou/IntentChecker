The paper presents IntentChecker, a development-time tool for Solidity that lets a developer declare numeric intent through two annotation types, @During and @Post, and validates that intent against the intervals produced by abstract interpretation, returning a tri-valued judgment (Satisfied, Warning, Violated) together with a risk score and a violated region. The tool extends the authors' prior work to a multi-contract single-transaction scope and adds an @IReturn annotation for interface return values. It is evaluated on 75 effective instances drawn from Web3Bugs, NumScout and a Greedy Contract dataset, where it returns Violated on 20 cases at a mean of 3.69 s per case, and is compared with ScType, GPTScan and NumScout along two design axes.

This is a technically competent, clearly written paper that also shows honesty about its limits. The central idea, that some numeric logic errors are intent-dependent and so call for developer-supplied intent rather than designer-supplied patterns, is well argued and worth publishing. My reservations are about the gap between what the evaluation demonstrates and what the paper claims for it. I read the evaluation as a demonstration of expressiveness and implementation feasibility rather than of practical bug-finding.

Below are outlined the strengths I found in this work:

I appreciated the problem formulation. The distinction between pattern-based detection and intent-based validation is drawn early and held throughout, and the observation that the same
arithmetic can be correct in one contract and a bug in another, separable only by reference to intent, is a genuine design-space argument.

The technical contribution is coherent and the formal treatment is thorough for a tool paper. The denotational semantics in 3.3, the tri-valued comparison primitive, and the truthiness and feasibility primitives are specified properly, and the @IReturn restriction to view and pure calls is well reasoned.

The two-axis framing in RQ3, who authors the detection criteria and when the tool runs, positions IntentChecker against existing tools without resting on a claim of superior detection.

The L1–L5 taxonomy in 4.3, and the value/algorithm by usable/unusable partition in particular, is a useful map of where an interval-domain intent checker reaches its limits. The finding that expressibility and awareness separate cleanly, with all L5 cases in usable cells and all unusable cases in L4, is a good piece of analysis.

Below are outlined my concerns, mainly about mismatches between claims and evaluation:

My main concern is that the RQ1 procedure explained in section 4.2 is to read the bug documentation, identify the buggy lines, and then write the intent annotations that capture the expected behaviour. Under that setup the annotator already knows where the bug is, what behaviour is intended, and which property to check. The experiment therefore shows that, given a correct intent specification, the engine can flag a violation in some buggy implementations. It does not show that the tool helps a developer find a bug they do not yet know about. I think this is a valid result, but it is a narrower claim than the framing suggests, and I would ask the authors to scope the contribution to it. The paper itself supports this reading when it describes the tool as an awareness tool rather than a detector (in 2.3 indeed the authors say ‘these two components position IntentChecker as an awareness tool rather than a detector: it does not pronounce “this is a bug” but instead brings the developer’s attention to the specific program points where the abstract execution cannot confirm the declared intent.’); my concern is that the evaluation narrative does not hold to that more modest description. The L5 category is in effect an admission of this dependency for 14 cases, but the same dependency on prior knowledge underlies all 20 successes.

IntentChecker returns Violated on 20 of 75 instances, about 26.7%. I would prefer the aggregate to carry more of the narrative weight than the failure taxonomy does. What sharpens this for me is that the 75 instances are already scoped to multi-contract single-transaction numeric errors (section 4.1), and then 14 of the 89 collected cases are removed by hand. Most of those exclusions are reasonable, but the effect is a set filtered towards the cases the tool targets, on which the result is still 20. I think a fairer framing would state plainly that IntentChecker can express and validate certain classes of numeric intent, that those classes account for 20 of 75 cases, and that the taxonomy explains why the rest fall outside current expressiveness or precision.

I totally agree with the authors that the tool is complementary rather than competitive, and the
scope-aware classification in section 4.4 is a fair attempt. My worry is that a table (to be precise, table 9) of ticks and N/As across four tools invites the reader to compare detection coverage anyway.
IntentChecker is evaluated on cases where a human supplied the intended property, the other tools as autonomous detectors, and the 55 missed cases do not enter the comparison. I would find a comparison along design dimensions (intent supplied versus inferred, development-time versus
audit-time, property checking versus pattern matching) cleaner than one that can be read as a coverage scoreboard.

The 3.69 s mean is computed over the 20 mitigated cases (Table 7). For a development-time tool, the latency the developer actually experiences includes the cases that end in Warning or never reach Violated, some of which involve the loop widening and fixpoint iteration that I would expect to be the slower paths. Reporting the distribution over all analysed cases would give a truer picture of usage cost.

In section 4.2 the authors write ‘The coverage across 7 of 8 patterns indicates that mitigability depends on whether the error is expressible as an intent violation, not on the error’s classification—suggesting that intent-based validation is pattern-agnostic in scope’. The underlying claim, that mitigability turns on expressibility rather than on the bug's classification, I find reasonable. The label does not follow from it, because expressibility is itself tied to pattern type: the authors' own
4.3 reports that the algorithm-level half is L4-heavy, 11 of 13. So certain patterns are harder to express by construction, which is the opposite of agnostic. Personally, I would drop or qualify the term.

The workflow requires developers to provide both intent annotations and debug ranges, yet the evaluation does not assess annotation effort, learning curve, or inter-annotator consistency. I view this as more than a usability concern, because annotation quality is a core determinant of the tool's effectiveness. In RQ1, annotations are written after reading the bug report and identifying the intended behaviour, effectively assuming that the correct intent can be formulated. As a result, it is difficult to separate the capabilities of the analysis engine from the evaluator's ability to reconstruct an appropriate specification. In my opinion a full developer study is not required for this work, but I would encourage the authors to more clearly acknowledge this dependency when discussing the current evaluation.
